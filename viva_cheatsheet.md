### Core Concepts Summary Table

| Concept | What We Did | Why | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Matrix representation** | Built a $44 \times 4$ Vandermonde matrix $A = [1, t, t^2, t^3]$ using normalized time coordinates $t \in [-1, 1]$ and target vector $b$ with NASA GISS temperature anomalies. | Formulated the cubic polynomial curve fitting task as a linear system $Ax = b$ to solve for coefficients $[a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3]^T$. | Overdetermined $44 \times 4$ system. Normalization bounded entries in $[-1, 1]$ to avoid numerical instability from huge powers ($1980^3$). Degree 3 chosen to capture acceleration while avoiding overfitting. |
| **RREF** | Programmed Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting from scratch in NumPy to compute $\text{rref}(A)$ and $\text{rref}([A \mid b])$. | To locate pivot positions, check column linear independence, and test whether an exact polynomial solution exists. | Found 4 pivots in $A$ (full column rank). In $[A \mid b]$, the 5th column has a pivot yielding row $[0 \; 0 \; 0 \; 0 \mid 1]$, proving the system is inconsistent. |
| **Rank** | Evaluated rank by counting non-zero pivot rows: $\text{rank}(A) = 4$ and $\text{rank}([A \mid b]) = 5$. Verified via `np.linalg.matrix_rank`. | To apply the Rouché–Capelli theorem to rigorously prove why an exact interpolating cubic cannot fit all 44 noisy data points. | $\text{rank}(A) < \text{rank}([A \mid b])$, confirming $b \notin \text{Col}(A)$. This mathematically justifies moving to least squares. |
| **Column/null space** | Analyzed fundamental subspaces of $A$: $\dim(\text{Col}(A)) = 4 \subset \mathbb{R}^{44}$ and $\text{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (nullity = 0). | To determine what temperature behaviors the model can represent and verify parameter uniqueness. | 4D column space captures smooth cubic trends; nullity = 0 guarantees a unique least squares coefficient solution $\hat{x}$. |
| **Gram-Schmidt** | Implemented Classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS) to decompose $A$ into $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{44 \times 4}$ with orthonormal columns and upper-triangular $R \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$. | To compute an orthonormal basis for $\text{Col}(A)$ and solve for coefficients stably without inverting $A^T A$. | Verified $Q^T Q = I_4$ and $A = QR$. Solved $R\hat{x} = Q^T b$ via back-substitution; verified solution matches normal equations exactly. |
| **Projection** | Computed the orthogonal projection $\hat{p} = A\hat{x} = Q Q^T b$ of temperature vector $b$ onto $\text{Col}(A)$, and residual $e = b - \hat{p}$. | The projection $\hat{p}$ is the geometrically closest vector in $\text{Col}(A)$ to $b$, minimizing the Euclidean distance $\|b - \hat{p}\|_2$. | $\hat{p}$ gives our fitted values. Verified orthogonality $A^T e \approx \mathbf{0}$ ($< 10^{-14}$) and minimum error $\|e\|_2 \approx 0.5971$. |
| **Least squares** | Solved the normal equations $(A^T A)\hat{x} = A^T b$ to find optimal cubic coefficients $\hat{x} = [a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3]^T$. | To minimize the sum of squared prediction errors $S = \|Ax - b\|_2^2$. | Coefficients: $a_0 \approx 0.5103$, $a_1 \approx 0.4325$, $a_2 \approx 0.1151$, $a_3 \approx -0.0203$. Achieved $R^2 \approx 0.8857$. |
| **Degree comparison** | Compared polynomial degrees 1 to 6 using $R^2$, adjusted $R^2$, residual norms, and 5-fold cross-validation MSE. | To mathematically justify degree 3 over simpler or more complex polynomial models. | Degree 1 is 86.74%, Degree 2 jumps to 88.55% (acceleration), Degree 3 adds +0.02% to 88.57%. Degrees 4–6 overfit (lower adj $R^2$, rising CV error). |
| **Interpolation** | Evaluated the fitted polynomial at 8 unobserved intermediate mid-years (e.g., 1983.5, 1995.5, 2020.5) inside $[1980, 2023]$. | To estimate historical temperature anomalies between annual records. | Produced smooth values tightly bounded by empirical data (e.g., $T(2000.5) \approx 0.49^\circ\text{C}$). High mathematical reliability. |
| **Extrapolation** | Evaluated the cubic model beyond 2023 up to 2050 at 5-year intervals (2025, 2030, ..., 2050). | To explore future warming trajectories and understand mathematical limits of polynomial projection. | Increments are nearly linear (~0.15°C/5yr); warming rate peaks at inflection ~2041–2042 ($t \approx 1.89$); curve turns over unphysically around 2112. |
| **Eigenvalues** | Formed sample covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{N-1}X_c^T X_c$ for centered $[t_{\text{norm}}, b]$, computed correlation $r$, and eigendecompositions. | To identify principal variance axes and evaluate scale-dependence of unstandardized covariance PCA. | Covariance $\lambda_1 = 0.4213$ (98.10%) is scale-dependent. Unit-free correlation is $r \approx 0.9313$ ($r^2 \approx 86.74\%$), proving strong correlation along upward trend. |
| **Diagonalization** | Factored covariance matrix into $C = P D P^T$, where $P$ has orthonormal eigenvectors and $D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$. | To decouple correlated time and temperature coordinates into independent orthogonal components. | Verified $C = P D P^T$ and $P^T P = I_2$ via `np.allclose`. Decoupled data into trend axis and orthogonal noise axis. |

### Sample Viva Questions & Student Answers

#### 1. Why did you normalize years to $[-1, 1]$ instead of using raw years like 1980 to 2023?
> **Student Answer:** If we plug raw calendar years into the Vandermonde matrix, the cubic column contains numbers like $2000^3 \approx 8 \times 10^9$. When we multiply $A^T A$, terms reach $t^6 \approx 6.4 \times 10^{19}$. That massive range of scales blows up the condition number past $10^{15}$, which causes catastrophic floating-point cancellation during Gaussian elimination. By mapping the years to $[-1, 1]$, every entry in $A$ stays between $-1$ and $1$, keeping the condition number small and the math numerically stable.

#### 2. Why write your own RREF and Gram-Schmidt functions instead of just calling `np.polyfit`?
> **Student Answer:** `np.polyfit` is a black box that hides the linear algebra. The whole objective of this mini-project is to show how polynomial fitting actually works from first principles—building the Vandermonde matrix, using RREF to show why an exact solution doesn't exist, using Gram-Schmidt to factor $A = QR$, and solving $R\hat{x} = Q^T b$ via back-substitution. Implementing them ourselves proves we understand the geometric mechanics behind the algorithms.

#### 3. What does your $R^2 \approx 0.886$ value actually mean?
> **Student Answer:** $R^2$ is the coefficient of determination. It tells us that about 88.57% of the total variance in observed temperature anomalies around their mean is captured by our cubic curve over time. The remaining ~11.43% corresponds to the squared residual norm $\|e\|^2$, which represents natural short-term climate noise like El Niño events or volcanic cooling that a smooth polynomial cannot capture.

#### 4. Can we actually trust your model's prediction of ~1.84°C in 2050?
> **Student Answer:** In our forecast window of 2025 to 2050, the predictions increase in nearly uniform increments of $\approx 0.15^\circ\text{C}$ every 5 years, so the curve behaves almost like a straight line. Because $a_3 = -0.0203$ is negative, the rate of warming actually peaks at an inflection point around 2041–2042 ($t \approx 1.89$), and the curve will eventually turn downward unphysically around year 2112 ($t \approx 5.16$).
> Furthermore, there is a baseline difference: NASA GISS uses the 1951–1980 baseline, which is already roughly $0.25^\circ\text{C}$ to $0.30^\circ\text{C}$ warmer than the pre-industrial period (1850–1900) referenced in the Paris Agreement. Crossing $+1.5^\circ\text{C}$ relative to pre-industrial happens around 2028–2030 ($+1.20^\circ\text{C}$ on NASA's scale), while $+1.54^\circ\text{C}$ in 2040 means roughly $+1.80^\circ\text{C}$ above pre-industrial.
> The honest limit is that cubics turn over unphysically far out, and our model contains no climate physics (greenhouse gas concentrations, radiative forcing, or ocean thermal inertia).

#### 5. What do the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix tell us about climate change?
> **Student Answer:** The covariance matrix of centered normalized time and temperature has eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \approx 0.4213$ (98.10%) and $\lambda_2 \approx 0.0081$ (1.90%). However, we must note that unstandardized covariance PCA is scale-dependent: time ($t \in [-1, 1]$, variance 0.357) and temperature in °C (variance 0.0725) have different units and numerical scales, so the first component naturally leans toward time.
> The unit-free, scale-invariant metric is the Pearson correlation coefficient: $r \approx 0.9313$, meaning $r^2 \approx 86.74\%$. This proves the two variables are strongly correlated along an upward trajectory, and a straight line explains ~87% of the variance. If we standardize both variables to unit variance (correlation matrix PCA), $\lambda_1 = 1.9313$, explaining 96.57% of standardized variance.

#### 6. How did you use Gram-Schmidt to solve the least squares problem?
> **Student Answer:** We used Classical Gram-Schmidt to decompose $A = QR$, where $Q$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^T Q = I_4$) and $R$ is upper triangular. Substituting $A = QR$ into the normal equations $(A^T A)\hat{x} = A^T b$ simplifies directly to $R\hat{x} = Q^T b$. Because $R$ is upper triangular, we solve it directly by back-substitution without ever forming or inverting $A^T A$, preserving numerical conditioning. We confirmed that this QR solution matches the normal equations solution exactly.

#### 7. Why did you choose polynomial degree 3 instead of degree 1 or degree 5?
> **Student Answer:** We justified degree 3 both conceptually and empirically:
> 1. Conceptually: Degree 1 assumes constant warming speed; degree 2 assumes uniform acceleration; degree 3 introduces an inflection point allowing acceleration to vary without high-order oscillations.
> 2. Empirically: We compared degrees 1 to 6 using $R^2$, adjusted $R^2$, and 5-fold cross-validation. Degree 1 achieves $R^2 = 86.74\%$. Degree 2 jumps to $88.55\%$, capturing acceleration. Degree 3 adds a tiny marginal gain (+0.02% to $88.57\%$), which is why $a_3 = -0.0203$ is small. Degrees 4 through 6 had declining adjusted $R^2$ ($87.67\% \to 87.18\%$) and rising cross-validation error ($0.0099 \to 0.0135$), proving they overfit to climate noise.

#### 8. Why did $\text{rank}(A) = 4$ while $\text{rank}([A \mid b]) = 5$?
> **Student Answer:** Matrix $A$ has 4 columns, so its column space is at most 4-dimensional. Because real temperature measurements contain noise and natural fluctuations, the 44 points do not lie on any single cubic curve. That means the observation vector $b$ doesn't lie in the 4D subspace $\text{Col}(A)$. When we augment $b$ into the matrix, it adds a 5th linearly independent column. By the Rouché–Capelli theorem, because $\text{rank}(A) < \text{rank}([A \mid b])$, the system $Ax = b$ is inconsistent and has no exact solution, which is why we must project $b$ onto $\text{Col}(A)$ using least squares.
